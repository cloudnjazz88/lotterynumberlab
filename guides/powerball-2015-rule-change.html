History

How the 2015 Powerball rule change reshaped the odds

Ten white balls added, nine red balls removed. The jackpot became two-thirds harder to win, small prizes became easier, and the first billion-dollar jackpot followed within months.

Published August 24, 2026 · figures computed from 2,312 drawings and the published game rules

On 4 October 2015 Powerball changed the contents of its two ball machines. The white-ball pool grew from 59 to 69; the red Powerball pool shrank from 35 to 26. The first drawing under the new rules was October 7, 2015, and the game has not changed since. It is the single most consequential rule change in the history of American lotteries.

Before and after, exactly

Before (5/59 + 1/35)After (5/69 + 1/26)
Jackpot odds1 in 175,223,5101 in 292,201,338

Two movements in opposite directions, both intentional. Adding white balls made the jackpot 66.8% harder to win. Removing red balls made the bottom tiers — where you only need the Powerball itself — substantially easier, which improved the overall chance of winning something by 21.9%, from 1 in 31.85 to 1 in 24.87.

The cost fell on the middle of the prize table. Matching all five white balls without the Powerball — the $1 million tier — became 2.27× rarer, from 1 in 5,153,633 to 1 in 11,688,054. As partial compensation, the match-4-plus-Powerball prize was raised from $10,000 to $50,000.

Why a lottery would make its jackpot harder

Jackpot size drives ticket sales far more powerfully than the probability of winning does. A harder jackpot rolls over more often, so the advertised prize climbs higher, which sells more tickets per drawing, which grows the prize faster still. Making the top prize less attainable and the small prizes more attainable is a deliberate design: it manufactures headline jackpots while keeping casual players entertained.

The rollover arithmetic

The mechanism is easiest to see through the probability that nobody wins a given drawing. If T tickets are sold and each has jackpot probability p, that chance is (1 − p)T. Here is the same ticket volume under both matrices:

Tickets sold in a drawingNo jackpot winner (old matrix)No jackpot winner (new matrix)
20 million89.2%93.4%
40 million79.6%87.2%
80 million63.3%76.0%
160 million40.1%57.8%
320 million16.1%33.4%

At a busy 160 million tickets, the old game had a 40.1% chance of rolling over; the new game has 57.8%. Compounded over consecutive drawings, that difference is the whole story: rolls last longer, so advertised jackpots reach altitudes that were previously almost unreachable.

What happened next

The evidence arrived quickly. On 13 January 2016 — barely three months after the change — Powerball paid $1.586 billion split between three tickets in California, Florida, Tennessee. It was the first lottery jackpot anywhere to pass $1 billion. Before October 2015, no US jackpot had ever exceeded $700 million; since then, more than a dozen have passed a billion, and Powerball holds the four largest prizes ever awarded.

Mega Millions read the same playbook. Its 2017 revamp took the white balls to 70 and pushed jackpot odds to 1 in 302,575,350, and the April 2025 change moved in the other direction for once — removing a single Mega Ball to improve jackpot odds to 1 in 290,472,336 and overall odds from 1 in 23.99 to 1 in 23.07 — while raising the ticket price to $5 and building a 2X–10X multiplier into every play.

Why we only analyse drawings after a matrix change

A rule change also breaks the drawing record in a way that matters for anyone doing statistics. Before October 2015, balls 60 to 69 did not exist in Powerball; a raw all-time frequency table therefore shows them as ice cold, for the simple reason that they were not in the machine. The same trap exists in Mega Millions, where the white-ball pool has been 50, 52, 56, 75 and now 70 balls at different times.

That is why every statistic on this site starts at the first drawing of the current matrix — October 7, 2015 for Powerball and October 31, 2017 for Mega Millions — giving 1,392 and 920 comparable drawings respectively. Mixing eras is the most common error in published lottery statistics, and it produces confident nonsense about "cold" numbers.

The part that did not change

Rule changes alter the odds. They do not alter the independence of drawings: the new matrix is reloaded from scratch every time, exactly like the old one. A ball's history still tells you nothing about tonight — it just tells you which era's rules were in force when it was drawn.
